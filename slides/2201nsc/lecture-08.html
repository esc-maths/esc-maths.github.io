Vector Spaces

Why Factorisation? (Real Numbers)

Prime factorisation is widely used in applications with real numbers:

  • LCM: Lowest Common Multiple (e.g., adding fractions)
  • HCF: Highest Common Factor (e.g., simplifying/cancelling fractions)
  • Dividing complicated numbers efficiently

Key Advantages:

  • Simpler, more manageable computational steps
  • A well-defined algorithmic process for humans or computers

Factorisation and Matrices

Which matrix structures are particularly convenient to work with?

  • Diagonal Matrices: e.g., $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2\end{pmatrix}$
  • Upper-Triangular Matrices: e.g., $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & -2\end{pmatrix}$
  • Lower-Triangular Matrices: e.g., $\begin{pmatrix}0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 5 & 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$
  • Orthogonal Matrices: Columns satisfy $v_i \cdot v_j = \delta_{ij}$

Matrix Factorisation Types

Matrix factorisations break $A$ into product forms of standard matrices:

Conventions: $U$ (Upper triangular), $L$ (Lower triangular), $D$ (Diagonal), $Q$ (Orthogonal)

LU Factorisation:

$$ A = LU $$

QR Factorisation:

$$ A = QR $$

Cholesky Factorisation:

$$ A = U^T U $$

SVD:

$$ A = Q_n \Sigma Q_m^T $$

LU Factorisation: Motivation

Row reduction provides a systematic approach to solving $A x = b$:

  1. Perform row operations on the augmented matrix $[A \mid b]$.
  2. After $k$ steps, the augmented system becomes: $$ [E_k \dots E_1 A \mid E_k \dots E_1 b] $$
  3. Objective: Convert $A$ into an upper triangular matrix $U$.
  4. The resulting system $[U \mid r]$ allows straightforward back substitution.

Back-Substitution: Unique Solution

System: $3x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 = -2$, $3x_1 + x_2 - x_3 = -5$, $3x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 = 2$

Row reduction to $[U \mid r]$:

$$ \begin{pmatrix}3 & 2 & -1 & -2 \\ 3 & 1 & -1 & -5 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 & 2\end{pmatrix} \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix}3 & 2 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 & -3 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4\end{pmatrix} $$

Back-substitution steps:

  • $2x_3 = 4 \implies \mathbf{x_3 = 2}$
  • $-x_2 = -3 \implies \mathbf{x_2 = 3}$
  • $3x_1 + 2(3) - (2) = -2 \implies 3x_1 + 4 = -2 \implies \mathbf{x_1 = -2}$

Back-Substitution: Family of Solutions

System: $3x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 + 2x_4 = -2$, $3x_1 + x_2 - x_3 + x_4 = -5$, $3x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 + 2x_4 = 2$

$$ \begin{pmatrix}3 & 2 & -1 & 2 & -2 \\ 3 & 1 & -1 & 1 & -5 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 & 2 & 2\end{pmatrix} \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix}3 & 2 & -1 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 & -1 & -3 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 0 & 4\end{pmatrix} $$

Back-substitution yields:

  • $2x_3 = 4 \implies \mathbf{x_3 = 2}$
  • $-x_2 - x_4 = -3 \implies \mathbf{x_2 = 3 - x_4}$
  • $3x_1 + 2(3 - x_4) - 2 + 2x_4 = -2 \implies 3x_1 + 4 = -2 \implies \mathbf{x_1 = -2}$

Question: What form does $E = E_m \dots E_1$ take where $U = E A$?


When Does $A = LU$ Hold?

$A = LU$ holds directly if row reduction requires no row swaps.

Writing $U = E A$ with lower triangular operations:

$$ U = EA = \begin{pmatrix}E_{11}\vec{a}_1 \\ E_{21}\vec{a}_1 + E_{22}\vec{a}_2 \\ \vdots \\ E_{m1}\vec{a}_1 + \dots + E_{mm}\vec{a}_n\end{pmatrix} $$

If $A = E^{-1} U$, then $E^{-1}$ must be lower-triangular.

This occurs if and only if the row reduction process uses no row swaps. In that case, $L = E^{-1}$ and $A = LU$.

Row Reduction Without Swaps

Augmenting identity $I \mid A$ during row reduction:

$$ \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0 & a_{11}&a_{12}&a_{13}&a_{14}\\ 0&1&0&0 & a_{21}&a_{22}&a_{23}&a_{24}\\ 0&0&1&0 & a_{31}&a_{32}&a_{33}&a_{34}\\ 0&0&0&1 & a_{41}&a_{42}&a_{43}&a_{44}\end{pmatrix} $$

Applying forward elimination (lower triangular multiplier steps):

$$ \rightarrow \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0 & a_{11}&a_{12}&a_{13}&a_{14}\\ E_{21}&1&0&0 & 0&b_{22}&b_{23}&b_{24}\\ E_{31}&E_{32}&1&0 & 0&0&c_{33}&c_{34}\\ E_{41}&E_{42}&E_{43}&1 & 0&0&0&d_{44}\end{pmatrix} = [E \mid U] $$

Taking inverse of $E$ gives $A = E^{-1}U = LU$.


PLU Factorisation & Permutations

If zero pivots occur, row swaps are required. We represent row swaps via a permutation matrix $P$.

Theorem: Any matrix $A$ can be written as $A = PLU$, where $L$ is lower-triangular, $U$ is upper-triangular, and $P$ is a permutation matrix.

Properties of Permutation Matrices $P$:

  • Square matrix with exactly one 1 per row and column (0s elsewhere)
  • Determinant is $\det(P) = \pm 1$
  • $P^m = I$ for some integer $m$, and $P^{-1} = P^T$ is also a permutation matrix

Uniqueness of PLU & PDL'U

Theorem: Any matrix $A$ has a unique factorisation $A = PLU$ where $U$ is in row-reduced echelon form.

Extended Form ($A = PDL'U$):

  • $P$ and $U$ are identical to the $PLU$ factorisation.
  • $L'$ is unit lower-triangular with 1s along the diagonal.
  • $D$ is a diagonal matrix containing row scaling factors.
  • Relation: $DL' = L$.

QR Factorisation: Column Space View

In $A x = b$, the system represents a linear combination of columns:

$$ \sum_i x_i a_i = x_1 a_1 + x_2 a_2 + \dots + x_n a_n = b $$

Solvability conditions:

  • If $b \notin \text{Col}(A) \implies$ No solution
  • If $b \in \text{Col}(A) \implies$ At least one solution
  • If $\text{Null}(A) \neq \{0\} \implies$ Infinite family of solutions

Benefits of Orthogonal Bases

If column vectors $a_i$ are orthonormal ($a_i \cdot a_j = \delta_{ij}$), finding coefficients $x_j$ is trivial:

$$ a_j \cdot b = a_j \cdot (x_1 a_1 + x_2 a_2 + \dots + x_n a_n) $$ $$ a_j \cdot b = x_1 (a_j \cdot a_1) + \dots + x_j (a_j \cdot a_j) + \dots = x_j $$

Alternatively, if $A$ is orthogonal ($A^T A = I$):

$$ A x = b \implies x = A^T A x = A^T b \implies x_j = a_j \cdot b $$

Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalisation

Constructing an orthonormal basis $\{\hat{v}_j\}$ from span $\{a_j\}$:

  1. $v_1 = a_1 \implies \mathbf{\hat{v}_1 = \frac{v_1}{\|v_1\|}}$
  2. Subtract projection onto $\hat{v}_1$: $v_2 = a_2 - (a_2 \cdot \hat{v}_1)\hat{v}_1 \implies \mathbf{\hat{v}_2 = \frac{v_2}{\|v_2\|}}$
  3. Subtract projections onto $\hat{v}_1, \hat{v}_2$: $v_3 = a_3 - (a_3 \cdot \hat{v}_1)\hat{v}_1 - (a_3 \cdot \hat{v}_2)\hat{v}_2 \implies \mathbf{\hat{v}_3 = \frac{v_3}{\|v_3\|}}$
  4. General step: $$ v_m = a_m - \sum_{i=1}^{m-1} (a_m \cdot \hat{v}_i) \hat{v}_i \implies \mathbf{\hat{v}_m = \frac{v_m}{\|v_m\|}} $$

QR Factorisation: Basis Construction

Gram-Schmidt vectors $\hat{v}_j$ expressed as linear combinations of $a_i$:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} | & | & | \\ \hat{v}_1 & \hat{v}_2 & \hat{v}_3 & \dots \\ | & | & | \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} | & | & | \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 & \dots \\ | & | & | \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_{11} & c_{12} & c_{13} & \dots \\ 0 & c_{22} & c_{23} & \dots \\ 0 & 0 & c_{33} & \dots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{pmatrix} $$

In matrix notation:

$$ Q = AC $$

where $Q$ has orthonormal columns and $C$ is an upper-triangular matrix.


Deriving the QR Form

From the Gram-Schmidt relation, $Q = AC$ where $C$ is upper-triangular.

  • If $C$ is invertible, its inverse $C^{-1}$ is also upper-triangular.
  • Rearranging $Q = AC$ gives: $$ A = Q C^{-1} = Q U $$ where $U = C^{-1}$ is an upper-triangular matrix.
Note: In general proofs of QR factorisation, $C$ does not strictly need to be invertible.

The QR Factorisation Theorem

Theorem (QR Factorisation): Any matrix $A$ can be factorised as: $$ A = QU \quad (\text{or } A = QR) $$ where $Q$ is an orthogonal matrix and $U$ (or $R$) is upper-triangular.

Non-Uniqueness of QR Factorisation:

The QR factorisation is not unique because multiple orthonormal bases can span the same space.

Example in $\mathbb{R}^3$: For any angle $\theta$,

$$ \{\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta \\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\} $$

forms a valid orthonormal basis.

Least-Squares via QR Factorisation

Minimize $\|y - Xk\|^2$. Standard normal equation solution:

$$ k_0 = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y $$

Substitute $X = QU$ and use $Q^T Q = I$:

$$ \begin{aligned} k_0 &= ((QU)^T (QU))^{-1} (QU)^T y \\ &= (U^T Q^T Q U)^{-1} U^T Q^T y \\ &= (U^T U)^{-1} U^T Q^T y \\ &= U^{-1} (U^T)^{-1} U^T Q^T y \\ \mathbf{k_0} &\mathbf{= U^{-1} Q^T y} \end{aligned} $$

Using QR factorisation simplifies least-squares into an elegant, computationally stable form!

Summary of QR Factorisation

Theorem: Any matrix $A$ can be factorised as $A = QU$ (or $A = QR$), where $Q$ is orthogonal and $U$ is upper-triangular.

Key Properties & Advantages:

  • Directly related to the column space of $A$.
  • Provides an explicit orthonormal basis for the column space.
  • Highly advantageous for regression and least-squares problems.
  • Offers superior numerical stability compared to standard LU decomposition.

Cholesky Factorisation

Theorem: Any real, symmetric, positive-definite matrix $A$ can be factorised as: $$ A = U^T U \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad A = L L^T $$ where $U$ is upper-triangular and $L = U^T$ is lower-triangular.

Proof Outline:

  1. Diagonalise $A = V D V^T$ with orthogonal $V$. Positive-definiteness implies eigenvalues $D_i > 0$.
  2. Define diagonal $S = D^{1/2}$, so $D = S^2 = S S^T \implies A = V S S^T V^T = (VS)(VS)^T$.
  3. Apply QR factorisation to $(VS)^T = QU \implies A = (QU)^T (QU) = U^T Q^T Q U = U^T U$.

Calculating Cholesky Factorisation

Command: U = chol(A)

Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Theorem: Any matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ can be written as: $$ A = Q_n \Sigma Q_m^T $$ where $Q_n$ ($n \times n$) and $Q_m$ ($m \times m$) are orthogonal matrices, and $\Sigma$ is an $n \times m$ diagonal matrix with non-negative real entries.

Notation Warning:

SVD is often written as $A = U \Sigma V^T$. Note that $U$ and $V$ in this context refer to orthogonal matrices, not upper-triangular matrices!

Key Insight: There is generally no direct connection between the eigenvalues of $A$ and its singular values!

SVD vs. $A^T A$ and $A A^T$

Comparing SVD components with $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ eigensystems:

  • Singular Values ($\Sigma$): Square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^T A$ (and $A A^T$). $$ \sqrt{\lambda(A^T A)} = \sqrt{\begin{pmatrix}20.2195 & 0 \\ 0 & 1.7805\end{pmatrix}} = \begin{pmatrix}4.4966 & 0 \\ 0 & 1.3343\end{pmatrix} = \Sigma $$
  • Right Vectors ($V$): Columns of $V$ are the eigenvectors of $A^T A$.
  • Left Vectors ($U$): Columns of $U$ are the eigenvectors of $A A^T$.

Non-Square SVD: Spectral Properties

Eigenvalues of $A^T A$ for the $2 \times 3$ matrix:

$$ \lambda(A^T A) = \{21.5156, \; 5.4844, \; 2.52 \times 10^{-15}\} $$ $$ \sqrt{\lambda(A^T A)} \approx \{4.6385, \; 2.3419, \; 0\} = \text{diag}(\Sigma) $$
Numerical Artifacts: Values like $2.52 \times 10^{-15}$ are floating-point limitations and represent exact zeroes.

SVD vs. Canonical (Diagonal) Form

  • Singular Values vs. Eigenvalues: $\Sigma$ entries are non-negative square roots of the non-zero eigenvalues of $A A^T$ and $A^T A$.
  • Rank & Ordering: For rank $r$, exactly $r$ singular values are non-zero, placed in descending order along the diagonal.
  • Dimensions: $Q_n$ ($n \times n$) and $Q_m$ ($m \times m$) differ in size for non-square matrices.
  • Orthonormality: Columns of $Q_n$ and $Q_m$ form orthonormal sets because they are eigenvectors of real symmetric matrices $A A^T$ and $A^T A$.
