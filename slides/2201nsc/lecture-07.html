Linearity & Vector Spaces

We can apply our general linear framework as long as the linearity condition makes sense:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\alpha x + \beta y) = \alpha \mathcal{L}(x) + \beta \mathcal{L}(y) $$

  • $\alpha x + \beta y$ must always exist for any scalars $\alpha, \beta$ and elements $x, y$.
  • The usual algebraic rules (commutativity, associativity, etc.) must apply.

👉 Any set of objects obeying these rules is called a vector space.



Why "Vector Space"?

Any point in 3D space can be written as a vector of coefficients: $$ \mathbf{c} = (c_1, c_2, c_3) = c_1 \mathbf{i} + c_2 \mathbf{j} + c_3 \mathbf{k} $$

We can maintain this coefficient representation for completely different mathematical objects using another basis $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\}$:

For polynomials of degree $\le 2$:

$$ P_2(x) = c_1 + c_2 x + c_3 x^2 \iff (c_1, c_2, c_3) $$

This provides a unified framework to analyze vectors, matrices, and functions.


What is a Vector Space?

A vector space is a collection of objects (vectors) equipped with:

  • A notion of vector addition
  • A notion of scalar multiplication

Closure Properties:

  • Adding two vectors produces another vector in the space.
  • Rescaling a vector produces another vector in the space.

Addition and scalar multiplication obey the 'usual' rules:

  • Associativity, commutativity, distributivity, identity, inverse.
  • Rules obeyed by vectors, matrices, and functions.

Formal Definition of a Vector Space

A real vector space $V$ is a nonempty set, with a notion of addition and a notion of scalar multiplication, whose elements satisfy the following axioms for any $\mathbf x,\mathbf y,\mathbf z \in V$ and $\alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{R}$:

  1. $\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y} \in V$ (Closure under addition)
  2. $\alpha\mathbf{x} \in V$ (Closure under scalar mult.)
  3. $\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{x}$ (Commutativity)
  4. $(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})+\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}+(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})$ (Associativity)
  5. $\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{x}$ (Additive identity)
  6. Each $\mathbf{x}$ has an element $-\mathbf{x}$ such that $\mathbf{x} + (-\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}$ (Additive inverse)
  7. $\alpha(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}) = \alpha\mathbf{x} + \alpha\mathbf{y}$ (Distributivity over vector addition)
  8. $(\alpha+\beta)\mathbf{x} = \alpha\mathbf{x} + \beta\mathbf{x}$ (Distributivity over scalar addition)
  9. $(\alpha\beta)\mathbf{x} = \alpha(\beta\mathbf{x})$ (Scalar associativity)
  10. $1\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}$ (Multiplicative identity)

  • Euclidean Spaces: $\mathbb R,$ $\mathbb{R}^2,$ $\mathbb{R}^3, \dots, \mathbb{R}^n$

Subspaces

To show a subset $S \subseteq V$ is itself a vector space, we only need to verify closure:

Definition: A non-empty subset $S \subseteq V$ is a subspace of $V$ if:

  1. $\alpha \,\mathbf x \in S$ whenever $\mathbf x \in S$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$
  2. $\mathbf x + \mathbf y \in S$ whenever $\mathbf x \in S$ and $\mathbf y \in S$

Note: A subspace is a complete vector space in its own right.



Subspace Example

Let $S = \left\{\mathbf x \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x_3 = 0\right\}$ be a subset of $\R^3$ $(S\subset \R)$.

For any $\mathbf x, \mathbf y\in S$ and $\alpha\in \R$:

1. Closure under addition:

$\mathbf x+\mathbf y $ $= (x_1, x_2, 0) + (y_1, y_2, 0) $ $= (x_1+y_1, x_2+y_2, 0)$ $ \in S$

2. Closure under scalar multiplication:

$\alpha \,\mathbf x $ $= \alpha (x_1, x_2, 0) $ $= (\alpha x_1, \alpha x_2, 0) $ $ \in S$

Hence $S$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$ ✅


Non-Subspace Example

Let $S = \left\{\mathbf x \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x_3 = 1\right\}$ be a subset of $\R^3$ $(S\subset \R)$.

For any $\mathbf x, \mathbf y\in S$ and $\alpha\in \R$:

1. Closure under addition:

$\mathbf x+\mathbf y $ $= (x_1, x_2, 1) + (y_1, y_2, 1) $ $= (x_1+y_1, x_2+y_2, 2)$ $ \notin S$

2. Closure under scalar multiplication:

$\alpha \,\mathbf x $ $= \alpha (x_1, x_2, 1) $ $= (\alpha x_1, \alpha x_2, \alpha) $ $ \notin S$

Hence $S$ is not a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$


Subspace Example: $2\times 2$ Matrices

Let $S$ be the set of $2\times 2$ matrices where $x_{21} = -x_{12}$.

For any $A, B\in S$ and $\alpha\in \R$:

1. Closure under addition:

$\begin{pmatrix}x_{11} & x_{12}\\ -x_{12} & x_{22}\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}y_{11} & y_{12}\\ -y_{12} & y_{22}\end{pmatrix} $ $= \begin{pmatrix}x_{11}+y_{11} & x_{12}+y_{12}\\ -(x_{12}+y_{12}) & x_{22}+y_{22}\end{pmatrix} $ $ \in S $

2. Closure under scalar multiplication:

$\alpha \begin{pmatrix}x_{11} & x_{12}\\ -x_{12} & x_{22}\end{pmatrix} $ $= \begin{pmatrix}\alpha x_{11} & \alpha x_{12}\\ -(\alpha x_{12}) & \alpha x_{22}\end{pmatrix} $ $ \in S $

Hence $S$ is a subspace of $\,\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$


The Null Space of a Matrix

Definition: The null space $N(A)$ of $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is the set of all solutions to the homogeneous system $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0.$ That is, $$N(A) = \left\{\mathbf x \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid A\mathbf x = 0\right\}.$$

Proof that $N(A)$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$:

  1. The zero solution always exists, so $N(A)$ is not empty: $ \mathbf{0} \in N(A)$.
  2. If $\mathbf x,\mathbf y \in N(A)$, then $\mathbf x+\mathbf y \in N(A)$:

    $ A(\mathbf x+\mathbf y) $ $ = A\mathbf x + A\mathbf y $ $ = 0 + 0 = 0 $

  3. If $x \in N(A)$, then $\alpha \mathbf x \in N(A)$:

    $ A(\alpha \mathbf x) $ $ = \alpha (A\mathbf x) $ $ = \alpha \cdot 0 = 0 $


Finding the Null Space

Find $N(A)$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$

Augmented matrix: $\; \left(\begin{array}{cccc|c}1 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0\\ 2 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0\end{array}\right)$ $ \rightarrow \left(\begin{array}{cccc|c}1 & 0 & -1 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 2 & -1 & 0\end{array}\right) $

From which we obtain: $ \;\;\left\{ \begin{array}{l} x_1 -x_3 +x_4 = 0 \\ x_2 + 2x_3 - x_4 = 0 \end{array}\right.\;\; $ $ \Ra\;\;\left\{ \begin{array}{l} x_1 = x_3 -x_4 \\ x_2 =- 2x_3 + x_4 \end{array}\right. $

Set as free variables $x_3 = \alpha$, $x_4 = \beta ,\,$ then $\, x_1 = \alpha - \beta, \; x_2 = -2\alpha + \beta$:

$ \mathbf x = \begin{pmatrix}x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \\ x_4\end{pmatrix} $ $ = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha -\beta \\ -2\alpha + \beta \\ \alpha \\ \beta\end{pmatrix} $ $ = \alpha \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix} + \beta \begin{pmatrix}-1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} $


Null Space Subspace Structure

Denote $\mathbf v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}-1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}$ $\Ra N(A) = \big\{\alpha \mathbf v_1 + \beta \mathbf v_2 \mid \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}\big\} $

For $\mathbf x, \mathbf y \in N(A)$, then $\mathbf x = \alpha_1 \mathbf v_1 + \beta_1 \mathbf v_2$ and $\mathbf y = \alpha_2 \mathbf v_1 + \beta_2 \mathbf v_2$:

$ \mathbf x+\mathbf y =\alpha_1 \mathbf v_1 + \beta_1 \mathbf v_2 + \alpha_2 \mathbf v_1 + \beta_2 \mathbf v_2$ $ = \left(\alpha_1+\alpha_2\right)\mathbf v_1 + \left(\beta_1+\beta_2\right)\mathbf v_2 $ $ \in N(A) $

For $k\in \R,\;$ $k\, \mathbf x = k \left(\alpha_1 \mathbf v_1 + \beta_1 \mathbf v_2\right)$ $= \left(k \alpha_1\right) \mathbf v_1 +\left(k \beta_1\right) \mathbf v_2$ $\in N(A)$

Therefore $N(A)$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^4$!


Solutions via Null Space

Theorem: If $\mathbf{x}_p$ is a solution to $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$, then the set of all solutions is $$\big\{\mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{v} \mid \mathbf{v} \in N(A)\big\}.$$

Proof: Let $\mathbf{x}_p$ be a solution of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ and let $\mathbf{v} \in N(A)$.

$\Ra\,A(\mathbf{x}_p+\mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{x}_p + A\mathbf{v}$ $= \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{0}$ $= \mathbf{b}$.

For any other solution $\mathbf{z}$ where $A\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{b}$:

$A(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}_p)$ $= A\mathbf{z} - A\mathbf{x}_p$ $= \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{b}$ $= \mathbf{0}$ $\implies (\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}_p) \in N(A)$.

In other words, $\,\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}_p = \mathbf{v}$ for some $\mathbf{v}\in N(A)$.

Therefore $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{v}$ for some $\mathbf{v}\in N(A)$. $\blacksquare$


Solving $A\mathbf x =\mathbf b$ with Null Space

Consider the system $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 0\\ 2 & 1 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\mathbf x = \begin{pmatrix}2\\ 3\end{pmatrix}.$

First find a particular solution: $\mathbf x_p = (1, 1, 0, 0)^T$

$\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 0\\ 2 & 1 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\mathbf x_p = \begin{pmatrix}2\\ 3\end{pmatrix}$

Full solution: Particular solution + Null space elements

$ x = \begin{pmatrix}1\\ 1\\ 0\\ 0\end{pmatrix} $ $+\, \alpha \begin{pmatrix}1\\ -2\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix} + \beta \begin{pmatrix}-1\\ 1\\ 0\\ 1\end{pmatrix}, \quad \alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{R} $


Linear Homogeneous ODEs

👉 A linear homogeneous ODE can be written as $L[f] = 0.$

Here $L$ is a map of $f$ that returns a linear combination of $f$ and its derivatives.

💡 Example: $\;L[f] = f'' + 3f' + 2f = 0$

Differentiation is a linear transformation, so $L[f ]$ is a linear map of $f.$

Solutions form the null space $N(L)$ of the linear operator $L$.

General ODE Solution Strategy:

  1. Find a particular solution $f_p$ to $L[f] = g$.
  2. Find homogeneous solutions $f_h \in N(L)$.
  3. General solution is $f = f_p + f_h$.

Bases Revisited

A basis allows us to express any vector in our space as a linear combination:

$$ v = c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_n v_n $$

This provides a general representation for vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

  • Subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^n$
  • Matrix spaces and function spaces

The Span of a Set of Vectors

Definition (Span): Let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ be vectors in a vector space $V$. The set of all linear combinations $$ \alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \dots + \alpha_n v_n $$ is called the span of $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$, denoted $\text{Span}(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$.

Spanning Sets

Definition (Spanning Set): The set $\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$ of vectors in $V$ is a spanning set for $V$ if and only if every vector $v \in V$ can be written as a linear combination of $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$: $$ v = \alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \dots + \alpha_n v_n \quad \forall v \in V $$

Proving Vectors Span a Space

Do $v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\4\end{pmatrix}$, $v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\3\end{pmatrix}$, and $v_3 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$ span $\mathbb{R}^3$?

We check if scalars $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3$ exist for any $(x, y, z)^T \in \mathbb{R}^3$ such that:

$$ \alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \alpha_3 v_3 = \begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix} $$

Expressing as a matrix system:

$$ \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1 \\ \alpha_2 \\ \alpha_3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix} $$

Proving Vectors Span a Space (Cont.)

Let $A \mathbf{\alpha} = \mathbf{x}$ where $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1\end{pmatrix}$:

$\det(A) = 1(1 - 0) - 2(2 - 0) = -3 \neq 0 \implies A^{-1} \text{ exists.}$

$$ \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1 \\ \alpha_2 \\ \alpha_3\end{pmatrix} = A^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix}-1 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -2 & -5 & 3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix} $$

Since unique coefficients $\alpha_i$ exist for any $x, y, z$, the vectors span $\mathbb{R}^3$.


Proving Functions Span a Space

Do $v_1 = 1 - x^2$, $v_2 = x + 2$, and $v_3 = x^2$ span $\mathcal{P}_3$?

We check if $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3$ exist for any $ax^2 + bx + c$:

$$ \alpha_1(1-x^2) + \alpha_2(x+2) + \alpha_3 x^2 = ax^2 + bx + c $$ $$ (\alpha_3 - \alpha_1)x^2 + \alpha_2 x + (\alpha_1 + 2\alpha_2) = ax^2 + bx + c $$

Matrix formulation:

$$ \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1 \\ \alpha_2 \\ \alpha_3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}a \\ b \\ c\end{pmatrix} $$

Proving Functions Span a Space (Cont.)

For system $A \mathbf{\alpha} = \begin{pmatrix}a \\ b \\ c\end{pmatrix}$:

$\det(A) = 1(0 - 1) = -1 \neq 0 \implies A^{-1} \text{ exists.}$

$$ \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1 \\ \alpha_2 \\ \alpha_3\end{pmatrix} = A^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}a \\ b \\ c\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -2 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a \\ b \\ c\end{pmatrix} $$

Since coefficients $\alpha_i$ exist for any $a, b, c$, these functions span $\mathcal{P}_3$.


Bases: Minimal Spanning Sets

Definition (Basis): The vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ in a vector space $V$ form a basis for $V$ if and only if:
  1. $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ are linearly independent
  2. $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ span $V$

Basis Examples

Standard Basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$:

$$ \left\{ e_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \; e_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \; e_3 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} \right\} $$

These vectors are linearly independent and span all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.


Alternative Bases for $\mathbb{R}^3$

Other valid bases for $\mathbb{R}^3$ include:

  • $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix} \right\}$
  • $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} \right\}$

Note: Only two vectors, e.g., $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} \right\}$, will not be enough to span $\mathbb{R}^3$.


Basis Size Limits & Null Space Basis

Four vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ cannot be linearly independent: $$ \left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} \right\} $$

Null Space Basis Example:

The set $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} \right\}$ forms a basis for $N\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$.


Standard Bases for Matrices & Polynomials

Standard Basis for $2\times 2$ Matrices ($\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$):

$$ \left\{ E_{11}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}, \; E_{12}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}, \; E_{21}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \; E_{22}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \right\} $$

Standard Basis for Polynomial Space $\mathcal{P}_n$:

$$ \{1, x, x^2, \dots, x^{n-1}\} $$

Important Results on Bases

  • If $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ spans $V$, then any subset of $V$ with more than $n$ vectors is linearly dependent.
  • If both $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ and $\{u_1, \dots, u_m\}$ are bases for $V$, then $n = m$.
  • If $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ is a basis for $V$, each vector $v \in V$ is a unique linear combination of $v_j$: $$ v = \sum_{j=1}^n c_j v_j $$

Dimension of a Vector Space

All bases for a vector space contain the exact same number of vectors.

Definition (Dimension): The dimension of a vector space $V$ is the number of non-zero vectors in any of its bases.

Example: $\dim(\mathbb{R}^3) = 3$, since its standard basis has 3 elements.


Dimension Examples

  • Matrix Space $\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$:
    Basis has 4 elements $\implies \dim(\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}) = 4$.
  • Polynomial Space $\mathcal{P}_4$ (degree $< 4$):
    Basis is $\{1, x, x^2, x^3\} \implies \dim(\mathcal{P}_4) = 4$.
  • Continuous Function Space $\mathcal{C}[0,1]$:
    Basis contains infinitely many polynomial terms $\{1, x, x^2, \dots\} \implies \dim(\mathcal{C}[0,1]) = \infty$.

Changing Bases Outside Point Spaces

Write $x^2 + 2$ in terms of the basis $\{1-x^2, \; x+2, \; x^2\}$:

$$ c_1(1-x^2) + c_2(x+2) + c_3(x^2) = x^2 + 2 $$ $$ (c_3 - c_1)x^2 + c_2 x + (c_1 + 2c_2) = x^2 + 2 $$
$$ \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} $$
$$ \begin{pmatrix}c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -2 & 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 0 \\ 3\end{pmatrix} $$

Check: $2(1-x^2) + 3(x^2) = 2 - 2x^2 + 3x^2 = x^2 + 2 \; \checkmark$


Changing Bases Outside Point Spaces

Write $x^2+2$ in terms of the basis $\{1-x^2, \; x+2, \; x^2\}$:

Set up the linear combination:

$$ c_1(1-x^2) + c_2(x+2) + c_3(x^2) = x^2 + 2 $$ $$ (c_3 - c_1)x^2 + c_2 x + (c_1 + 2c_2) = x^2 + 2 $$

Express as a linear system:

$$ \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} $$

Solve by matrix inversion:

$$ \begin{pmatrix}c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -2 & 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 0 \\ 3\end{pmatrix} $$

Check: $2(1-x^2) + 3(x^2) = 2 - 2x^2 + 3x^2 = x^2 + 2 \quad \checkmark$


Column and Row Spaces

Returning to the matrix system $A x = b$:

Definition (Column Space): The column space of a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is the subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$ spanned by the columns of $A$.
Definition (Row Space): The row space of a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is the subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ spanned by the rows of $A$.

Column & Row Spaces: Examples

Consider matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$:

Column Space: Set of all linear combinations of columns:

$$ \alpha_1 \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} + \alpha_2 \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} + \alpha_3 \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1 + \alpha_3 \\ \alpha_2 + \alpha_3\end{pmatrix} $$

$\text{Column Space of } A = \{y : y = Ax \text{ for any } x\}$

Row Space: Set of all linear combinations of rows:

$$ \alpha_1 \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} + \alpha_2 \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1 & \alpha_2 & \alpha_1 + \alpha_2\end{pmatrix} $$

$\text{Row Space of } A = \{y : y = xA \text{ for any } x\}$


Consistency & Invertibility Results

  • A linear system $A x = b$ is consistent iff $b$ lies in the column space of $A$: $$ A x = x_1 a_1 + x_2 a_2 + \dots + x_n a_n = b $$
  • $A x = b$ is consistent for all $b$ iff the column space of $A$ is $\mathbb{R}^m$.
  • An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is invertible iff its column vectors form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$.
    (Invertible $\iff$ unique solution to $A x = b \iff$ columns span and are independent in $\mathbb{R}^n$)

Rank, Nullity & The Rank-Nullity Theorem

Rank of $A$: Dimension of row space $=$ dimension of column space $=$ number of lead variables in RREF($A$).

Nullity of $A$: Dimension of null space $=$ number of free variables in RREF($A$).

Theorem (Rank-Nullity Theorem): If $A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix, then: $$ \text{rank}(A) + \text{nullity}(A) = n $$

Proof idea: The $n$ columns in RREF($A$) are partitioned into lead variables (rank) and free variables (nullity).


Finding Spaces in Practice: Example 1

For matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$:

  1. Row Reduce: $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & -1\end{pmatrix}$
  2. Column Space Basis: Lead columns from original matrix $A$:
    $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} \right\} \implies \dim = 2 \quad (\text{rank} = 2)$
  3. Row Space Basis: Non-zero rows from RREF:
    $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & -1 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 & 2 & -1\end{pmatrix} \right\} \implies \dim = 2$
  4. Null Space Basis: $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} \right\} \implies \text{nullity} = 2$

Finding Spaces in Practice: Example 2

For matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$:

  1. Row Reduce: $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix} \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$
  2. Column Space Basis: $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix} \right\} \implies \dim = 2 \quad (\text{rank} = 2)$
  3. Row Space Basis: $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix} \right\} \implies \dim = 2$
  4. Null Space Basis: $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix} \right\} \implies \text{nullity} = 1$

Solvability of $Ax = b$: Example 1

Does $A x = b$ always have a solution for $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$?

  1. Rank $= 2$ and Nullity $= 2$.
  2. $\text{Rank } 2 \implies \dim(\text{Column Space}) = 2$.
  3. Column space is in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Since its dimension is 2, it spans all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  4. Conclusion: Any $b \in \mathbb{R}^2$ can be reached, so $A x = b$ always has a solution.
  5. Since nullity $= 2$, there is a 2-dimensional family of solutions for each $b$.

Solvability of $Ax = b$: Example 2

Does $A x = b$ always have a solution for $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$?

  1. Rank $= 2$ and Nullity $= 1$.
  2. $\text{Rank } 2 \implies \dim(\text{Column Space}) = 2$.
  3. Column space lives in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Because $\dim = 2$, it forms a 2D plane subspace and does not fill $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  4. Conclusion: Not every $b \in \mathbb{R}^3$ can be reached $\implies A x = b$ does not always have a solution.
  5. When a solution exists, nullity $= 1$ yields a 1-dimensional family of solutions.
