A Brief Story of $\large \pi$ and $\large e$

A Brief Story of $\large \pi$

A very brief overview

Where does $\large \pi$ come from?



Origin of $\large \pi$

  • Babylonians (~1900-1600 BCE) used $\pi$ ≈ 3.125.
  • Egyptians (Rhind Papyrus) used $\pi$ ≈ 3.1605.
  • Archimedes (250 BCE): $\pi$ between 3.1408 and 3.1429.
  • Symbol “$\pi$” introduced by William Jones in 1706; adopted by Euler.


$\large \pi$ Through History

  • Used in ancient architecture and astronomy.
  • Computed to more than 1 trillion digits in modern times.
  • Appears in unexpected places: probability, Fourier series, prime numbers.

Common Uses Today

  • Geometry: circle formulas, surface areas, volumes.
  • Trigonometry: sine, cosine, periodic functions.
  • Physics: waves, oscillations, relativity.
  • Engineering: signal processing, simulations, structural analysis.
  • Computer graphics: rotations, animations, transformations.

Fun Facts

  • $\large \pi$ is irrational — its decimal expansion never ends or repeats.
  • $\large \pi$ is also transcendental — impossible to express as a root of a polynomial.
  • March 14 (3/14) is celebrated as Pi Day around the world.

Why $\large \pi$ Matters

A universal constant appearing throughout mathematics, nature, science, and technology.

The Story of $\large e$

A very brief overview

Origin of $\large e$

  • First appeared in studies of compound interest in the 1600s.
  • Jacob Bernoulli discovered the limit \( \left(1 + \dfrac{1}{n}\right)^n \).
  • Leonhard Euler gave the constant its name “$e$” and formalised its properties.

What is $\large e$?

  • The base of natural logarithms.
  • Approximately 2.71828… (irrational and transcendental).
  • The unique number for which the derivative of \( e^x \) is itself.

Common Uses Today

  • Exponential growth and decay (population, radioactivity, interest).
  • Calculus: differential equations, integrals, limits.
  • Probability: normal distribution, Poisson processes.
  • Complex analysis: Euler's formula \( e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0 \).

Fun Facts

  • Shown to be irrational by Euler (1737) and transcendental by Hermite (1873).
  • Appears naturally in combinatorics, physics, and information theory.

Why $\large e$ Matters

The natural base that describes change, randomness, and the structure of continuous processes.
