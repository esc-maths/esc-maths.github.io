Index laws

Index laws, also called exponent rules, help us to simplify expressions involving powers. They are particularly useful when multiplying or dividing powers with the same base, raising a power to another power, and working with zero, negative or fractional exponents.

Multiplying and dividing powers

When multiplying powers with the same base, add the exponents.

\[ a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}. \]

When dividing powers with the same base, subtract the exponent in the denominator from the exponent in the numerator.

\[ \frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}, \qquad a \neq 0. \]

For example,

\[ x^3 \times x^4 = x^{3+4} = x^7. \]

Similarly,

\[ \frac{y^5}{y^2} = y^{5-2} = y^3. \]

Notice that we add the exponents when multiplying and subtract them when dividing. In both cases, the base remains unchanged.

Power of a power

When a power is raised to another power, multiply the exponents.

\[ (a^m)^n = a^{mn}. \]

For example,

\[ (x^2)^3 = x^{2 \times 3} = x^6. \]

Power of a product

When a product is raised to a power, apply the exponent to each factor inside the brackets.

\[ (ab)^n = a^n b^n. \]

For example,

\[ \begin{aligned} (3a^2)^2 &= 3^2 (a^2)^2 \\ &= 9a^4. \end{aligned} \]

Another example is

\[ \begin{aligned} (2x^3)^2 &= 2^2 (x^3)^2 \\ &= 4x^6. \end{aligned} \]

Zero and negative indices

A power with an exponent of zero is equal to one, provided the base is nonzero.

\[ a^0 = 1, \qquad a \neq 0 \]

For example,

\[ 7^0 = 1 \qquad \text{and} \qquad (-1234)^0 = 1. \]

A negative exponent indicates the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power. Therefore,

\[ a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}, \qquad a \neq 0. \]

For example,

\[ \begin{aligned} 10^{-2} &= \frac{1}{10^2} \\ &= \frac{1}{100}. \end{aligned} \]

Similarly,

\[ x^{-3} = \frac{1}{x^3}, \qquad x \neq 0. \]

Rational indices

A rational index is an exponent expressed as a fraction. An exponent of \(\tfrac{1}{n}\) represents an \(n\)-th root. That is,

\[ a^{\frac{1}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a}. \]

More generally,

\[ a^{\frac{m}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a^m}. \]

For example,

\[ 4^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sqrt{4} = 2. \]

Similarly,

\[ 8^{\frac{1}{3}} = \sqrt[3]{8} = 2. \]

These rules connect fractional exponents with the familiar operations of finding square roots, cube roots and higher-order roots.

Worked example: Simplifying an expression

Simplify the following expression using the index laws:

\[ \left(\frac{2a^2b}{b^3}\right)^3 \div \left(\frac{16a^5}{ab^7}\right)^2. \]

Assume that \(a \neq 0\) and \(b \neq 0\).

Step 1: Simplify the expressions inside the brackets

Using the law for dividing powers,

\[ \frac{2a^2b}{b^3} = \frac{2a^2}{b^2} \]

and

\[ \frac{16a^5}{ab^7} = \frac{16a^4}{b^7} \]

Therefore, the original expression becomes

\[ \left(\frac{2a^2}{b^2}\right)^3 \div \left(\frac{16a^4}{b^7}\right)^2. \]

Step 2: Apply the power of a product and power of a power laws

\[ \frac{8a^6}{b^6} \div \frac{256a^8}{b^{14}}. \]

Step 3: Rewrite the division as multiplication by the reciprocal

\[ \frac{8a^6}{b^6} \times \frac{b^{14}}{256a^8}. \]

Step 4: Simplify the numerical coefficients and apply the index laws

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{8a^6b^{14}}{256a^8b^6} &= \frac{1}{32}a^{6-8}b^{14-6} \\ &= \frac{1}{32}a^{-2}b^8. \end{aligned} \]

Finally, using the law for negative indices,

\[ \boxed{\frac{b^8}{32a^2}}. \]
